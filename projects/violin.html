Violin & Orchestral Leadership

More than a decade of violin shaped how I approach leadership, preparation, teamwork, and long-term improvement. From concertmaster and chamber roles in high school to selective performances at university, music remains one of the strongest influences on how I work.

Overview

I started violin when I was eight years old after hearing Tchaikovsky during a car ride home from school and asking my mom if I could take lessons. What began as curiosity became a major part of my life throughout elementary and high school, eventually growing into orchestral leadership, chamber music, solo performance, festivals, recitals, and community involvement.

Music demanded consistency long before I understood those habits in an academic or engineering context. Preparing difficult repertoire over months, rehearsing under deadlines, adapting in real time to an ensemble, and taking responsibility for other musicians taught me a great deal about discipline, communication, initiative, creativity, and problem solving.

Orchestral leadership

I served as concertmaster of the London Youth Symphony, under London Symphonia, leading an orchestra of more than 50 young musicians through rehearsals and concerts featuring major orchestral and symphonic works. The role extended well beyond sitting first chair: I tuned the orchestra, helped establish ensemble direction from the first violin section, supported concert preparation, led first-violin sectionals during selected rehearsals, communicated with the conductor, and helped translate musical direction into consistent execution across the ensemble.

At school, I was concertmaster of my high-school ensembles, including senior orchestra, symphony, and chamber orchestra. I was also heavily involved in the broader music program as Co-President of Music Council, balancing performance responsibilities with organization, communication, and support for the school music community.

Those roles taught me that leadership in a technical group is rarely about giving the most instructions. It is often about preparation, setting a standard, listening closely, communicating clearly, and making it easier for everyone around you to perform well.

Chamber music & training

I performed as first chair and first violin in a range of chamber ensembles, including octets, sextets, quintets, quartets, and trios. These groups performed in Kiwanis festivals, recitals, and through the Young Artist Pre-College Academy. Chamber music was especially formative because there is nowhere to hide: every player carries an individual part while also being responsible for the balance, phrasing, timing, and direction of the whole group.

I studied violin under Irene Tandberg and participated in Western University's WISE scholarship program, where I attended recitals and masterclasses with professional soloists. Exposure to advanced performers and teachers reinforced the importance of deliberate practice: identifying the precise source of a problem, isolating it, testing a different approach, and repeating that process until the result becomes reliable.

Solo performance & service

One of my major high-school performances was the Mendelssohn Violin Concerto at Centennial Hall in Grade 12 with my school orchestra. I also performed as the final soloist in a London Community Orchestra winter concert while still in high school.

Selected solo performance with the London Community Orchestra.

I also volunteered at L'Arche, performing violin for community members with intellectual disabilities. That experience was a reminder that music is not only about technical achievement or formal performance; it can also be a direct way to connect with people and contribute to a community.

University & reflection

University has left much less time for the practice schedule I maintained in high school, so music now plays a smaller but still meaningful role in my life. I continue to perform when I can. At the University of Toronto, I perform annually at Engineering Science's Nocturne event, and I am currently playing violin and fiddle for the Engineering Drama Society's production of Come From Away.

I am grateful for what violin taught me long before I began applying those same habits to engineering: creativity under constraints, sustained focus, meeting performance deadlines, solving small problems methodically, taking initiative, leading without overpowering a team, and building habits through repetition. Most importantly, it taught me that difficult work becomes manageable when improvement is treated as a long process rather than a single performance.
