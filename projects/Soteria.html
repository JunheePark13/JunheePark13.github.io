Praxis II · Team design project

Soteria Cycling Safety System

A bike-mounted right-hook warning prototype that combines a rear-facing camera, OpenCV-based vehicle detection, GPS intersection gating, a mobile application, and physical phone/camera mounts to alert cyclists when a vehicle enters the blind-spot region near an intersection.

01 · Problem framing

Targeting the moment a cyclist cannot easily see

Soteria focused on right-hook collisions: a vehicle travels beside or slightly behind a cyclist, enters the cyclist’s blind spot, and then turns right across the cyclist’s path at an intersection.

The team chose a bike-mounted warning system rather than an infrastructure intervention or a system that required equipment on every car. That kept the concept retrofit-oriented: the cyclist carries the sensing and alert hardware, so the design can operate independently of intersection reconstruction or vehicle adoption.

Hazard locationRear / side blind spotA rear-facing camera watches the region from which an overtaking vehicle can become a right-hook threat.
Hazard timingIntersections onlyGPS is used to determine when the cyclist is at or near an intersection so continuous vision processing is not required for the entire ride.
User interfaceAlert the cyclist immediatelyThe phone is mounted in front of the rider and provides the warning when the system detects a relevant vehicle.
ImplementationRetrofit the bicycleCamera, phone, wiring, and fixtures are attached to the bike rather than requiring changes to road infrastructure.

02 · System architecture

Use location to decide when vision is worth running

The core design decision was to couple GPS context with computer vision. Vehicle detection matters most when a cyclist is approaching an intersection, so the system uses location as a gate: near an intersection, the rear camera and OpenCV pipeline monitor the blind-spot region; away from intersections, continuous tracking is unnecessary.

This reduced unnecessary processing and battery use while keeping the sensing focused on the scenario the team was actually trying to mitigate.

1ContextGPS intersection gate

The app determines whether the cyclist is at or approaching an intersection before enabling the hazard-monitoring workflow.

2SenseRear-facing camera

A camera mounted behind the rider looks toward adjacent traffic in the cyclist’s blind-spot region.

3InterpretOpenCV vehicle detection

The vision pipeline identifies vehicles in the camera view and communicates a detection to the mobile application.

4AlertPhone notification

The handlebar-mounted phone provides an immediate warning to the cyclist through the app when the risk condition is detected.

System idea: sensing is not valuable simply because it can run continuously. GPS narrows the operating window to the location where a right-hook conflict becomes relevant, while computer vision provides the local vehicle awareness GPS cannot.

03 · Mechanical & human integration

The software only works if the hardware lives on a bicycle

Soteria was not just a vision script. The team also had to package the sensing and warning hardware around an actual rider: the camera needed a stable rear-facing position, the phone needed to remain visible and reachable at the handlebars, and the wiring had to follow the bicycle without interfering with moving parts.

The prototype therefore combined the camera module with a rear mounting solution and a front phone fixture. The phone acted as both the rider-facing interface and the connection point for the camera/app workflow.

Camera placementRear-facing field of viewPositioned to observe vehicles approaching from behind and beside the cyclist rather than looking forward.
Phone placementFront, visible, reachableThe rider receives the warning without needing to turn around or remove a hand to check a separate device.
WiringRoute around moving interfacesThe design work considered attachment and cable routing so the added electronics would not interfere with normal bicycle operation.
ModularityReplaceable componentsThe design was treated as an assembly of separable camera, holder, phone, and alert components rather than one monolithic product.

04 · Requirements engineering

05 · Prototype testing

Test the alert pathway without creating the collision scenario

For reaction time, the team used a proxy test rather than putting a cyclist into a real right-hook conflict. Participants watched a riding/driving simulation and responded to a randomly triggered alert by pressing a button. Ten participants completed three trials each; the final average response time was 420 ms.

Detection distance was tested separately. Across ten trials, the prototype detected the target at distances ranging from 25 to 35 m; the minimum observed distance was 25 m, exceeding the 15 m requirement.

420 msFinal average alert-to-response time
25–35 mObserved detection-distance range
25 mMinimum detection distance used for pass/fail

What these results demonstrate

The tests verify the prototype against the team’s defined proxy requirements. They do not establish real-world collision reduction, false-positive performance across all road environments, or production-level computer-vision robustness. Those would require substantially broader field testing.

07 · Limitations & next steps

Move from a working prototype toward a road-ready system

The prototype demonstrated that the sensing, location gating, rider alert, and physical mounting ideas could be integrated into one bike-mounted system. The largest remaining gap is robustness outside a controlled prototype environment.

HardwareWireless, weather-resistant cameraRemoving the wired phone-camera connection would reduce cable hazards and make the package easier to install on different bicycles.
AlertsBroader haptic / visual feedbackA vibrating wearable or stronger external alert could complement the phone and improve accessibility without forcing the rider to watch a screen.
VisionFalse-positive / false-negative testingRoad-ready computer vision would need evaluation across lighting, weather, vehicle types, occlusion, and different intersection geometries.
LocationMore robust intersection triggeringGPS gating would benefit from tuned geofences and additional context so processing starts early enough without running unnecessarily for long periods.

Takeaway: Soteria became a systems-design problem: sensing alone was not enough. The useful prototype came from connecting computer vision, location context, a mobile interface, physical mounting, requirements, and verification into one rider-facing workflow.
