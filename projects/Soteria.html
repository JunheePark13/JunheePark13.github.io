Praxis II · Engineering design process

Soteria Cycling Safety System

A Praxis II team project developing a bike-mounted right-hook warning prototype using a rear-facing camera, OpenCV-based vehicle detection, GPS intersection gating, a mobile application, and physical phone/camera mounts. The course emphasized the full engineering design process: research, requirements, documentation, verification, iteration, and communication—not only the final prototype.

01 · Problem framing

Targeting the moment a cyclist cannot easily see

Soteria focused on right-hook collisions: a vehicle travels beside or slightly behind a cyclist, enters the cyclist’s blind spot, and then turns right across the cyclist’s path at an intersection.

Our team chose a bike-mounted warning system rather than an infrastructure intervention or a system that required equipment on every car. That kept the concept retrofit-oriented: the cyclist carries the sensing and alert hardware, so the design can operate independently of intersection reconstruction or vehicle adoption.

Hazard locationRear / side blind spotA rear-facing camera watches the region from which an overtaking vehicle can become a right-hook threat.
Hazard timingIntersections onlyGPS determines when the cyclist is at or near an intersection so continuous vision processing is not required for the entire ride.
User interfaceAlert the cyclist immediatelyThe phone is mounted in front of the rider and provides the warning when the system detects a relevant vehicle.
ImplementationRetrofit the bicycleCamera, phone, wiring, and fixtures are attached to the bike rather than requiring changes to road infrastructure.

02 · System architecture

Use location to decide when vision is worth running

The core system decision was to couple GPS context with computer vision. Vehicle detection matters most when a cyclist is approaching an intersection, so location acts as a gate: near an intersection, the rear camera and OpenCV pipeline monitor the blind-spot region; away from intersections, continuous tracking is unnecessary.

This reduces unnecessary processing and battery use while keeping the sensing focused on the scenario the design is intended to mitigate.

1ContextGPS intersection gate

The app determines whether the cyclist is at or approaching an intersection before enabling the hazard-monitoring workflow.

2SenseRear-facing camera

A camera mounted behind the rider looks toward adjacent traffic in the cyclist’s blind-spot region.

3InterpretOpenCV vehicle detection

The vision pipeline identifies vehicles in the camera view and communicates a detection to the mobile application.

4AlertPhone notification

The handlebar-mounted phone provides an immediate warning to the cyclist through the app when the risk condition is detected.

System idea: GPS supplies context about where the hazard matters; computer vision supplies local awareness of the vehicle itself. Combining the two avoids running the highest-cost sensing continuously.

03 · Mechanical & human integration

The software only works if the hardware lives on a bicycle

Soteria was not only a vision script. The team also had to package sensing and warning hardware around an actual rider: the camera needed a stable rear-facing position, the phone needed to remain visible and reachable at the handlebars, and the wiring had to follow the bicycle without interfering with moving parts.

The prototype combined the camera module with a rear mounting solution and a front phone fixture. The phone acted as the rider-facing interface and the connection point for the camera/app workflow.

Camera placementRear-facing field of viewPositioned to observe vehicles approaching from behind and beside the cyclist rather than looking forward.
Phone placementFront, visible, reachableThe rider receives the warning without needing to turn around or check a separate device.
WiringRoute around moving interfacesAttachment and cable routing were considered so the added electronics would not interfere with normal bicycle operation.
ModularityReplaceable componentsThe design was treated as an assembly of separable camera, holder, phone, and alert components rather than one monolithic product.

04 · Praxis II design process

The deliverable was the reasoning as much as the hardware

Praxis II placed strong emphasis on showing how an engineering decision was reached—not simply presenting a finished object.

For Soteria, that meant documenting the problem evidence, researching existing approaches and standards, translating broad goals such as safety and accessibility into requirements, comparing design directions, justifying component choices, defining verification methods, collecting test data, and communicating limitations. The prototype was one output of that process rather than the entire project.

ResearchBuild an evidence baseRight-hook collision context, existing systems, standards, reaction-time literature, sensing feasibility, and human-alert research were documented before finalizing the design direction.
RequirementsTurn goals into measurable criteriaSafety, legality, accessibility, detection distance, response time, sound, light, and implementation constraints were converted into explicit evaluation criteria.
VerificationDesign the test before claiming successReaction-time and detection-distance procedures were documented with materials, preparation, steps, repetition, and pass/fail logic.
CommunicationMake the design auditableThe final one-pager condensed the system, rationale, integration, and next steps into a visual design artifact while the supporting documentation preserved the detailed reasoning.

Praxis II takeaway: a credible engineering design is not just a prototype that works once. It is a traceable chain from problem framing → evidence → requirements → concepts → decisions → verification → documented limitations.

05 · Requirements engineering

Translate “make cyclists safer” into things the prototype can be tested against

The team’s framework covered safety, legality, accessibility, and practical implementation. Two requirements were particularly useful for prototype verification because they could be measured directly.

The broader documentation also considered limits on alert sound and light, legal constraints, accessibility, wiring safety, bicycle attachment requirements, and component replaceability. Those considerations helped prevent the team from optimizing detection performance in isolation from the product that a cyclist would actually use.

06 · Prototype verification

Test the requirements that were defined earlier

The verification plan used controlled proxy tests rather than claiming real-world crash prevention. For reaction time, participants responded to the prototype alert while viewing a simulated driving/riding scenario. For detection distance, the camera/detection system was evaluated across repeated vehicle approaches with a predefined distance threshold.

420 msAverage response across the documented reaction-time participants
25 mMinimum vehicle detection distance across 10 final trials
10 trialsDetection-distance runs used for the final minimum result

The detection trials ranged from 25 to 35 m. Reporting the minimum rather than the best observation kept the comparison conservative against the 15 m requirement. The 420 ms reaction-time average likewise remained below the project’s 500 ms criterion.

What these tests do—and do not—show

They verify prototype-level response and detection requirements under the documented test conditions. They do not establish a reduction in real-world collision probability, robustness across every lighting/weather condition, production-level computer-vision accuracy, or long-term battery performance.

07 · Design communication

Condensing the process into one system view

The final one-pager communicates the design purpose, bike integration, computer-vision module, GPS activation logic, phone alert, electric horn concept, design values, right-hook scenario, and future improvements. In Praxis II, this communication artifact mattered because the design had to be understandable and defensible beyond the team that built it.

08 · Limitations & next steps

From course prototype to a more credible field system

The prototype demonstrated the intended architecture and met the two highlighted verification criteria, but substantial work would remain before treating it as a road-ready safety product.

CameraMove toward a wireless, weather-resistant moduleThis would reduce cable routing around the bicycle and lower dependence on the phone for camera power/connectivity.
AlertsAdd redundant feedback channelsExternal lighting or a wearable/haptic alert could complement the phone and improve accessibility.
Computer visionValidate across real operating conditionsLighting, occlusion, weather, different vehicle classes, false positives, and false negatives would need structured field testing.
EnergyMeasure the benefit of GPS gatingThe design rationale is to reduce unnecessary processing near non-intersection road segments; battery testing would quantify that benefit.

Engineering takeaway: Soteria was valuable because it connected software, sensing, mechanical integration, human factors, requirements, testing, and documentation in one design process. The final prototype mattered, but the stronger lesson was learning to make each decision traceable to evidence and a verification plan.
