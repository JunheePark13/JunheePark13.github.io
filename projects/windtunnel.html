Independent · Experimental aerodynamics

Desktop Wind Tunnel

Designed and built a compact suction wind tunnel for desktop-scale aerodynamic experiments. The project became an exercise in engineering tradeoffs when the first flow-straightening honeycomb—optimized for low blockage—proved too fragile to manufacture and install reliably.

01 · Design brief

A useful wind tunnel that still had to fit on a desk

The goal was to make a compact experimental platform with a clear test section, meaningful flow conditioning, and enough velocity for qualitative aerodynamic work using a single readily available fan.

That created an immediate constraint: every improvement in flow quality—larger settling volume, longer contractions, screens, denser flow straighteners—also increased size or pressure loss. The tunnel therefore had to be designed as a system rather than as a collection of individually ideal components.

FootprintCompact before perfectA 200 × 200 mm settling chamber would have met the initial 4:1 area-ratio target, but 180 × 180 mm was selected to keep the tunnel practical on a desktop.
Flow conditioningHoneycomb firstThe honeycomb was prioritized to suppress lateral velocity components and swirl. Fine screens were omitted initially to avoid adding more resistance to a fan-limited system.
Fan placementPull rather than pushThe fan was placed downstream so it draws air through the conditioned tunnel rather than injecting fan wake directly into the upstream test flow.
PackagingShort, sealed flow pathCompact transitions and sealed joints reduce leakage and internal recirculation while keeping the full assembly manageable.

02 · Flow architecture

Condition, accelerate, test, recover

The tunnel follows the sequence settling chamber → contraction → test section → diffuser → fan → guard. The 180 × 180 mm settling chamber feeds a 160 mm contraction into the 100 × 100 mm test section. Downstream, the diffuser expands toward the 120 mm fan.

Settling chamber180 × 180 mm cross-section; sized as a desktop compromise against the original ~4:1 contraction target.
Honeycomb≈10 mm cells, ≈75 mm flow length; used to suppress lateral velocity components and swirl.
Contraction160 mm long, reducing from 180 × 180 mm to 100 × 100 mm.
Test section100 × 100 mm cross-section, 120 mm long, with transparent walls for observation.
Fan120 × 120 × 38 mm variable-speed fan positioned downstream in suction. The design target was up to roughly 7 m/s; that value is a target rather than a calibrated measurement.

Design principle: flow quality and available pressure are coupled. Adding a conditioner only helps if the fan can still drive useful flow through the extra resistance.

03 · Honeycomb V1

The low-blockage design failed before aerodynamic testing

V1 used thin honeycomb walls to preserve open area and minimize obstruction. That was attractive from a flow perspective: less solid material in the inlet should reduce blockage and the associated pressure penalty.

The design failed at the manufacturing and integration stages. The thin walls were unreliable during FDM printing, and the resulting mesh was too fragile to install into the tunnel confidently. The first iteration demonstrated that minimizing resistance alone did not produce a usable flow conditioner.

V1 · Low blockage

Thin walls preserved open area and reduced the amount of solid material presented to the flow.

Failure · Too fragile

Printing reliability and physical handling became limiting constraints before the insert could serve as a practical tunnel component.

What changed: the design criterion moved from “minimize aerodynamic obstruction” to “minimize obstruction subject to being printable, installable, and durable enough to test.”

04 · Honeycomb V2

Accepting more resistance to make the conditioner usable

V2 increased wall thickness and prioritized structural robustness. The redesign produced a honeycomb that could be fabricated, handled, and integrated into the settling chamber without the fragility of V1.

The improvement was not free. Thicker walls reduce open area and therefore increase blockage and resistance. In a compact tunnel driven by a single fan, that penalty competes directly with available test-section velocity.

V1

Lower blockage and lower expected resistance, but insufficient manufacturing and integration robustness.

V2

Higher blockage and resistance, but robust enough to fabricate, install, and use in the assembled tunnel.

System-level result: V2 is not better in every metric. It is the better engineering solution because it exchanges some aerodynamic efficiency for the manufacturability and durability required for the tunnel to function at all.

05 · Fabrication & integration

Closing the loop from CAD to hardware

The final assembly combines the V2 flow-straightening insert with the contraction, transparent test section, diffuser, and downstream suction fan. The build made the coupling between subsystem decisions tangible: honeycomb strength affected printability and installation; wall thickness affected blockage; blockage consumed fan pressure capability; and limited fan pressure constrained how much additional flow conditioning could be added.

06 · Testing & next steps

The hardware works; aerodynamic characterization comes next

The tunnel has been assembled and operated, validating the mechanical integration and the V2 honeycomb’s practicality. I separate that result from aerodynamic validation: operation demonstrates a functioning experimental platform, but it does not by itself establish test-section uniformity, turbulence intensity, or calibrated airspeed.

Mechanical integrationSettling chamber, contraction, clear test section, diffuser and fan assembled into one flow path
V2 survives useRedesign resolves the fabrication and handling failure that prevented V1 from being practical
Quantification remainsVelocity profile, pressure drop and flow quality should be measured before stronger aerodynamic claims

The next useful experiments are a test-section velocity traverse, pressure-loss comparison across the honeycomb, and flow-uniformity checks with and without additional screens. Those measurements would quantify the aerodynamic cost of the V2 wall-thickness decision and show whether the fan has enough pressure margin for another conditioning stage.

Takeaway: the decisive iteration was not simply a CAD refinement. It was recognizing that aerodynamic efficiency, manufacturability, structural robustness, and fan pressure capability all have to close simultaneously for the experiment to exist.
