Desktop Wind Tunnel

Designed and built a compact suction wind tunnel for desktop-scale aerodynamic experiments. The project became an exercise in engineering tradeoffs when the first flow-straightening honeycomb—optimized for low blockage—proved too fragile to manufacture and install reliably.

01 · Design brief

A useful wind tunnel that still had to fit on a desk

The goal was not to build a miniature research facility. It was to make a compact experimental platform with a clear test section, meaningful flow conditioning, and enough velocity for qualitative aerodynamic work using a single readily available fan.

That created an immediate constraint: every improvement in flow quality—larger settling volume, longer contractions, screens, denser flow straighteners—also increased size or pressure loss. The tunnel therefore had to be designed as a system rather than as a collection of individually “ideal” components.

FootprintCompact before perfectA 200 × 200 mm settling chamber would have met the initial 4:1 area-ratio target, but 180 × 180 mm was selected to keep the tunnel practical on a desktop.
Flow conditioningHoneycomb firstThe honeycomb was prioritized to suppress lateral velocity components and swirl. Fine screens were omitted initially to avoid adding more resistance to a fan-limited system.
Fan placementPull rather than pushThe fan was placed downstream so it draws air through the conditioned tunnel rather than injecting fan wake directly into the upstream test flow.
PackagingShort, sealed flow pathCompact transitions and sealed joints reduce leakage and internal recirculation while keeping the full assembly manageable.

02 · Flow architecture

Condition, accelerate, test, recover

The tunnel follows the standard sequence settling chamber → contraction → test section → diffuser → fan → guard. The 180 × 180 mm settling chamber feeds a 160 mm contraction into the 100 × 100 mm test section. Downstream, a diffuser expands toward the 120 mm fan to recover pressure before the suction stage.

Settling chamber180 × 180 mm cross-section; sized as a desktop compromise against the original ~4:1 contraction target.
Honeycomb≈10 mm cells, ≈75 mm flow length; used to suppress lateral velocity components and swirl.
Contraction160 mm long, reducing from 180 × 180 mm to 100 × 100 mm.
Test section100 × 100 mm cross-section, 120 mm long, with transparent walls for observation.
Fan120 × 120 × 38 mm variable-speed fan positioned downstream in suction; the original design target was up to roughly 7 m/s, not yet presented here as a calibrated measurement.

Design principle: flow quality and available pressure are coupled. Adding a conditioner only helps if the fan can still drive useful flow through the extra resistance.

03 · Honeycomb V1

The aerodynamically attractive version failed the manufacturing test

The first honeycomb pushed toward thin cell walls to preserve open area and minimize blockage. Aerodynamically, that is appealing: thinner walls obstruct less of the inlet and should impose less pressure loss.

In practice, V1 exposed the weakness of optimizing only one objective. The thin walls were unreliable during FDM printing, and the finished insert was too fragile to handle and integrate into the tunnel without damage. A component that offers low resistance but cannot survive fabrication and assembly is not a usable flow conditioner.

V1 · Low blockage

Thin walls preserved open area and reduced the amount of solid material presented to the flow.

Failure · Too fragile

Printing reliability and physical handling became the limiting constraints before aerodynamic performance could even be evaluated.

What changed: the design criterion moved from “minimize aerodynamic obstruction” to “minimize obstruction subject to being printable, installable, and durable enough to test.”

04 · Honeycomb V2

Accepting more resistance to get a component that works

V2 increased the honeycomb wall thickness and prioritized structural robustness. The redesign printed more reliably and produced an insert that could be handled and installed without the same fragility as V1.

The improvement was not free. Thicker walls reduce open area and increase blockage, which means more flow resistance and pressure loss across the conditioner. In a compact tunnel driven by a single fan, that directly competes with achievable test-section velocity.

V1

Lower blockage, lower expected pressure loss, but insufficient manufacturing and integration robustness.

V2

Higher blockage and resistance, but robust enough to fabricate, install, and use as part of the assembled tunnel.

This was the key engineering decision of the build: V2 is not “better” in every metric. It is better at the system level because it converts an aerodynamically cleaner but unusable concept into a manufacturable component with a known aerodynamic penalty.

05 · Fabrication & integration

Turning the CAD stack into an actual flow path

The final assembly combines the printed flow-straightening insert with the contraction, transparent test section, diffuser, and downstream suction fan. The clear test section keeps the experiment observable, while the compact external shell provides the sealed geometry needed to pull air through the full tunnel rather than around it.

The build also made the interaction between component-level decisions visible: honeycomb strength affected printability and installation; wall thickness affected blockage; blockage affected the fan’s available flow; and the fan choice constrained how much additional conditioning could be added upstream.

06 · Testing & next steps

The build works; the next step is to characterize how well

The tunnel has been assembled and operated, which validates the mechanical integration and the V2 honeycomb’s practicality. I am deliberately separating that result from aerodynamic validation: the current evidence demonstrates a functioning experimental platform, not yet a calibrated low-turbulence facility.

Mechanical integrationSettling chamber, contraction, clear test section, diffuser and fan assembled into one flow path
Honeycomb V2 survives useRedesign resolved the fabrication and handling failure that prevented V1 from being practical
Quantification remainsVelocity profile, pressure drop and turbulence quality should be measured before stronger aerodynamic claims

The next useful experiments are a test-section velocity traverse, pressure-loss comparison across the honeycomb, and flow-uniformity checks with and without additional screens. Those measurements would quantify the cost of the V2 wall-thickness decision and show whether the fan has enough pressure margin for another stage of conditioning.

Takeaway: the most important iteration was not a prettier CAD model. It was learning that aerodynamic optimization, manufacturability, structural robustness, and fan pressure capability all have to close simultaneously for the experiment to exist.
