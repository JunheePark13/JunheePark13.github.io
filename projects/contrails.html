Industry-sponsored contrail research across three UTIAS propulsion groups

The broader program is a joint UTIAS effort involving Professor Clinton P. T. Groth's Computational Fluid Dynamics & Propulsion group and Professor Ömer L. 01 · Experimental validation

Fixing a geometry-driven 40% error before increasing fidelity

The initial planar model was useful for solver development but consistently overpredicted downstream centreline temperature. Changing inlet turbulence intensity and length scale did not remove the 36–43% error, indicating that the main limitation was the entrainment physics of the planar representation rather than a tunable turbulence constant.

I replaced it with a five-degree axisymmetric wedge and progressed the solver from rhoPimpleFoam to reactingFoam with chemistry disabled for compressible air–water-vapour transport. The validated case reproduced the available centreline measurements with a 1.42% mean absolute percentage error across six evaluated stations and a 2.64% maximum error.

Water-vapour transport was separately checked for bounded mass fractions and physically sensible plume spreading. It was not presented as independently validated because corresponding experimental H₂O concentration data were unavailable.

02 · Turbofan-relevant parameter study

Quantifying bypass cooling versus vapour dilution

The validated model was extended to the tunnel's core → nozzle wall → bypass → nozzle wall → ambient configuration rather than treating the problem as a single isolated jet. I ran a nine-case matrix spanning bypass ratios of approximately 2.50, 5.95, and 10.0 at bypass temperatures of 240, 270, and 300 K.

Colder bypass flow consistently cooled the plume faster and moved ice-supersaturation onset upstream. At BPR ≈ 10, reducing bypass temperature from 300 K to 240 K shifted the first centreline location with Si > 1 from 0.1600 m to 0.1123 m — about 47.7 mm upstream. Increasing bypass ratio produced an additional upstream shift while also increasing dilution of core-derived water vapour.

03 · Three-dimensional RANS & mesh design

Building a purpose-designed 14.27-million-cell LES mesh

A full 3D domain was required to permit azimuthal instabilities and vortex breakdown. I wrote a Python mesh generator for a structured cylindrical O-grid, using a central square to avoid the axis singularity and retaining annular regions for the core, bypass-ready geometry, ambient flow, and tunnel-wall treatment.

Instead of uniformly refining the entire tunnel, I used the developed SST-RANS field to guide LES resolution. The engineering metric combined the integral turbulence length scale, Lt ≈ √k/(0.3ω), with local cell size Δ = Vcell1/3; regions with fres = Lt/Δ < 5 were targeted for additional refinement around the expanding core–ambient shear layer.

04 · Large-eddy simulation & high-performance computing

Resolving the unsteady mixing that RANS averages away

LES was introduced only after the lower-order cases had established the geometry, thermophysical model, species transport, boundary treatment, and HPC workflow. The production and diagnostic cases ran on SciNet's Trillium cluster using 192 MPI ranks across three nodes, Scotch decomposition, restartable run segments, and adaptive timestepping with a maximum Courant number of 0.3.

The SGS investigation included WALE, Smagorinsky, and dynamicKEqn; the current density-based route uses dynamicKEqn. After establishing a bounded pure-air flow solution, water vapour was restored conservatively as a one-way-coupled passive mass fraction initialized from the developed RANS H₂O field.

05 · Numerical problem solving

Turning a persistent LES failure into a controlled solver investigation

The most difficult part of the project was a travelling velocity–pressure runaway in pressure-based reactingFoam and rhoPimpleFoam LES when velocity shear and thermal/density shear were present together. I isolated the problem systematically rather than tuning one setting at a time.

Variables testedMesh topology and refinement, inlet regularization, convection schemes, timestep controls, and multiple SGS closures.
Control casesVelocity shear alone and thermal/density shear alone remained stable over their test windows, while the coupled RANS solution was also stable.
Solver resultDensity-based rhoCentralFoam with central-upwind fluxes remained bounded over the tested interval and supported passive-H₂O continuation.

The controls weakened species transport, one defective mesh cell, boundary-condition type, and any single SGS model as complete explanations. The density-based comparison does not prove that solver architecture is the sole cause because the formulation also changes the energy and mixture treatment, but it provided the first tested 3D LES route that remained stable with the combined velocity and thermal/density gradients.

Engineering outcome

Validated physics, scalable simulation infrastructure, and a path to contrail statistics

The summer progressed from a geometry-limited planar model to a 1.42% mean thermal-validation error, a nine-case bypass study, a full 3D structured RANS framework, and a 14.27-million-cell RANS-informed LES running across 192 MPI ranks. The numerical-debugging campaign then converted repeated LES failure into a controlled solver comparison and a bounded density-based continuation with passive water-vapour transport.

Engineering scope demonstrated: experimental validation, compressible CFD, turbulence modelling, species transport, parametric analysis, structured mesh generation, high-performance computing, and systematic numerical debugging — applied to an industry-sponsored aerospace propulsion problem.

The next research step is to extend the LES through a statistically useful sampling interval and evaluate resolved fluctuations, temporal spectra, SGS-energy behaviour, and joint temperature–water-vapour distributions before computing instantaneous supersaturation statistics. Stability is a prerequisite; it is not itself LES validation.
