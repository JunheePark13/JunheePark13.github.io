Research program

From mean jet mixing to fluctuation-resolved contrail physics

This Pratt & Whitney Canada-sponsored research program at the University of Toronto Institute for Aerospace Studies brings together the research groups of Professors Swetaprovo Chaudhuri, Ashwani K. Gupta Gülder, and David Groth. My summer work focused on the CFD side of the program: building, validating, and progressively increasing the fidelity of an OpenFOAM framework for contrail-relevant exhaust mixing.

The thermodynamics make the problem strongly dependent on turbulence. As hot, water-vapour-rich exhaust entrains cold ambient air, both temperature and vapour concentration decrease. Saturation vapour pressure, however, decreases much more rapidly with temperature, so instantaneous turbulent mixing can create localized supersaturated pockets even while the plume is being diluted. A correct mean temperature field is therefore necessary, but it is not enough—the eventual target is the joint fluctuation field of temperature and water vapour.

Why LES: RANS/URANS can validate geometry, thermodynamics, and mean plume decay at much lower cost, but statistical averaging suppresses the fluctuations and coherent structures that control the tails of the temperature–vapour distribution. LES spatially filters the equations and resolves much of that turbulent motion directly.

Model hierarchy & validation

Diagnosing geometry, then validating the mean thermal field

I began with progressively more representative compressible jet models before spending the computational cost required for LES. A planar model consistently overpredicted downstream centreline temperature, with mean errors of roughly 40%; varying inlet turbulence did not remove the discrepancy. That pointed to the entrainment physics of the geometry rather than a tunable turbulence parameter.

Replacing the planar representation with a five-degree axisymmetric wedge corrected the mixing behaviour. Using reactingFoam with chemistry disabled as a compressible multicomponent air–water-vapour solver, the final wedge case reproduced the available centreline temperature measurements with a 1.42% mean absolute percentage error and 2.64% maximum error.

Water-vapour transport was checked for bounded mass fractions, mixture consistency, and physically sensible plume spreading and decay. Those checks establish numerical consistency; they are not presented as independent experimental validation of the H₂O field.

Turbofan-relevant parameter study

Quantifying bypass-ratio and bypass-temperature effects

I extended the validated axisymmetric framework with an annular bypass stream and ran a nine-case matrix: bypass ratios of approximately 2.50, 5.95, and 10.0, each at bypass temperatures of 240, 270, and 300 K. This separated two competing effects of bypass flow—faster plume cooling and stronger water-vapour dilution.

The temperature effect dominated the developing-plume supersaturation response over the investigated region. Colder bypass flow moved the first centreline location with Si > 1 upstream, and increasing bypass ratio produced the same qualitative shift. At BPR ≈ 10, reducing bypass temperature from 300 K to 240 K moved onset from x = 0.1600 m to x = 0.1123 m, almost 48 mm upstream.

Three-dimensional framework

3D RANS, structured O-grid design, and RANS-informed refinement

The validated axisymmetric model was then carried into a full three-dimensional cylindrical domain. I developed a structured O-grid and used the converged RANS solution to determine where LES resolution was most valuable. Rather than refining the entire tunnel uniformly, the mesh follows the expanding core–ambient shear layer where turbulent production, entrainment, and scalar gradients are concentrated.

Refinement criteria based on the developed RANS field were used to concentrate cells around the turbulent length scales and the growing shear layer. After repeated mesh-quality checks and redesign, the final production mesh contained exactly 14,272,512 cells, with 128 azimuthal divisions and 808 axial cells.

Large production and diagnostic cases were run on the SciNet Trillium cluster using 192 MPI ranks across three nodes, with OpenMPI/Slurm parallelization and adaptive timestepping constrained by a maximum Courant number of 0.3.

LES solver development

Isolating a compressible LES instability instead of tuning around it

The hardest part of the summer became a travelling velocity–pressure runaway in the pressure-based LES calculations. Rather than treating it as a single bad case, I built a diagnostic matrix to isolate its cause: mesh revisions, inlet regularization, convection schemes, RANS-to-LES initialization, filter-width changes, and WALE, Smagorinsky, and dynamicKEqn subgrid closures were all tested.

The key control cases showed that neither velocity shear nor thermal/density shear alone reproduced the same failure. The instability appeared when both were present together in the pressure-based LES, while the corresponding SST-RANS calculation remained stable. That narrowed the problem away from a single SGS model, species transport, or one isolated mesh defect and toward the pressure–density–energy coupling of the LES formulation.

Moving to the density-based rhoCentralFoam formulation with a Kurganov central-upwind flux produced the first tested 3D LES route that remained bounded with the combined velocity and thermal/density gradients. Passive H₂O transport was then reintroduced so the calculation retained the scalar information required for contrail-relevant thermodynamic post-processing.

Contrail-relevant post-processing

Reconstructing the thermodynamic state behind supersaturation

The LES is ultimately a tool for resolving the instantaneous thermodynamic states experienced by the turbulent plume. From each cell's temperature, H₂O mass fraction, and absolute pressure, the post-processing converts mass fraction to mole fraction, determines water-vapour partial pressure, and computes saturation ratios over ice and liquid water.

The statistical target is deliberately broader than a single contour: instantaneous and mean Si, centreline and radial profiles, fluctuation variance, probability-density functions, and joint temperature–water-vapour PDFs. Those joint distributions matter because contrail inception can be controlled by localized tails of the turbulent scalar field even when the mean state is not supersaturated.

Current physical scope: the framework diagnoses thermodynamic supersaturation. It does not yet model soot activation, explicit ice-particle populations, latent heat, or vapour depletion through condensation/deposition.

What the summer established

A validated mean-flow baseline and a defensible route toward fluctuation-resolved physics

The project progressed from a geometry-induced validation failure to a 1.42% mean thermal error, then used that validated model to investigate bypass effects, build a full 3D RANS framework, design a 14.27-million-cell RANS-informed LES mesh, and systematically isolate the failure mode of the original pressure-based LES route.

The most important lesson is methodological: matching a mean field is necessary, but it does not establish that the fluctuation physics is correct. The lower-order models provide the validation anchor; the density-based LES provides a workable path toward the instantaneous temperature–water-vapour statistics needed for contrail inception. Extending the bounded LES into a statistically developed sampling interval is the next step.
